Lipoproteins, glycogen, sugars, hormones, fats and cholesterol

VLDL, LDL and HDL carry fats and cholesterol in the blood; glycogen is the sugar store inside liver and muscle cells. Pick one to explore it, or follow sugar, insulin and the lipoproteins through the whole body. Below them: the sugars glucose, fructose and sucrose, the hormones insulin and glucagon, the fats, and the cholesterol molecule.

The messengers that steer where the sugar goes: insulin stores it after a meal, glucagon releases it between meals.

Sterols

Cholesterol is a different kind of lipid: not made of fatty acids but of four joined rings. See it in 3D, attach a fatty acid, and find where it rides in LDL and HDL.
About “good” and “bad” cholesterol. HDL and LDL carry the same cholesterol molecule; the nicknames describe where the particles take it. Too many LDL particles (more exactly, all ApoB particles: LDL, VLDL remnants, Lp(a)) cause plaque in artery walls. A higher HDL level goes with lower risk, but drugs that only raise HDL have not prevented heart attacks, so HDL is better seen as a sign of good health than as a target. Glycogen is different: it is stored sugar (chains of glucose) inside liver and muscle cells, not a fat carrier. Simplified educational picture, not medical advice.

    Size and density compared

    Bigger particles carry more fat (triglyceride), which is lighter than water, so they are less dense. Hover for numbers.

    What VLDL is

    VLDL is the liver’s fat delivery truck. It is a large ball, about 30 to 80 nm across, that carries fat (triglycerides) from the liver through the blood to the body.

    • Outside: one layer of phospholipids with some free cholesterol. Their water-loving heads face the blood.
    • Inside: an oily core that is mostly triglycerides, with some cholesteryl esters. Triglycerides make up about half to two thirds of the particle’s weight (sources give 50 to 70%).
    • Proteins: exactly one ApoB-100, wrapped around the particle, plus smaller proteins: ApoC-II, ApoC-III and ApoE.
    • Protein is only about a tenth of its weight, which is why it is so light (“very low density”).

    Made by the liver

    • The liver builds each VLDL around one ApoB-100 and loads it with fat (the helper protein MTP does the loading), then sends it into the blood.
    • Most of the fat comes from fatty acids the liver takes up from the blood (released by fat tissue), plus fat from leftover meal particles.
    • A smaller part is fat the liver makes from extra sugar (de novo lipogenesis). It is small on a normal diet and rises when you eat more carbohydrate (especially fructose) than you burn.
    • Right after a meal, insulin normally slows the liver’s VLDL output. With insulin resistance and a fatty liver, that brake fails and the liver makes too many large VLDL.

    Its proteins

    • ApoB-100 The backbone: one huge protein (4,536 amino acids) per particle. It stays on for life, from VLDL to LDL.
    • ApoC-II The “on” switch for lipoprotein lipase, the enzyme that takes the fat out.
    • ApoC-III A brake: it slows lipoprotein lipase and slows the liver’s uptake of what is left. More ApoC-III goes with higher triglycerides.
    • ApoE A handle for the liver: it lets the liver grab and take up the shrunken particles (IDL).

    VLDL leaves the liver with some ApoC and ApoE and picks up more from HDL in the blood. As it shrinks it hands them back.

    Unloading the fat

    • Lipoprotein lipase sits on the inner wall of the capillaries in fat tissue, muscle and the heart. It is made by fat and muscle cells and carried to the inside of the capillary by a helper protein (GPIHBP1).
    • When a VLDL brushes past, its ApoC-II switches the enzyme on. The enzyme splits triglycerides into fatty acids.
    • Fat tissue stores the fatty acids again as fat; muscle and heart burn them for energy.
    • As fat leaves, the particle shrinks; extra surface (phospholipids, ApoC) moves over to HDL.

    VLDL → IDL → LDL

    • A VLDL that has lost much of its fat is an IDL (intermediate-density lipoprotein), about 25 to 35 nm.
    • About half of the IDL is taken up by the liver (using ApoE).
    • The rest is trimmed further, also by hepatic lipase in the liver, loses its ApoE and becomes LDL: small, mostly cholesterol, still with its one ApoB-100.
    • So most LDL in the blood started life as a VLDL.

    Why it matters

    • In a fasting blood test, most of the triglyceride is carried in VLDL.
    • VLDL, its remnants (IDL) and LDL all carry ApoB. ApoB particles up to about 70 nm can enter the artery wall, so smaller VLDL and remnants can add to plaque too, not only LDL.
    • With insulin resistance, the liver overproduces large VLDL. That leads to high triglycerides, more remnants, smaller LDL and lower HDL.

      What glycogen is

      Glycogen is how the body stores sugar. It is one huge, tree-like molecule made only of glucose, built around a small protein in the middle, glycogenin.

      • Glucose units are joined in chains by α-1,4 bonds.
      • About every 8 to 12 glucose a new chain branches off with an α-1,6 bond (sources give 8 to 13).
      • The chains form layers (tiers): inner chains carry two branches each; outer chains carry none.
      • The outer chains have many free ends. Enzymes add or remove glucose there, so the body can store or release sugar quickly.

      Not a lipoprotein. HDL, LDL and VLDL carry fats and cholesterol in the blood. Glycogen is made from glucose and stays inside cells (mainly liver and muscle cells).

      How big it is

      • One granule (a β-particle) is usually about 20 to 30 nm across. The full model with 12 tiers would be about 42 to 44 nm with about 55,000 glucose.
      • Most real granules are smaller: in muscle the average is about 25 nm, roughly 7 tiers.
      • In the liver, many β-particles clump into larger rosettes (α-particles), up to about 200 to 300 nm.
      • For scale: an LDL particle is about 18 to 25 nm, HDL about 7 to 12 nm.

      Where it is stored

      • Liver: about 100 g when full (about 6 to 8% of the liver’s weight). It is for the whole body: the liver turns it back into glucose and sends it into the blood.
      • Muscles: about 400 g in all (1 to 2% of muscle weight; more with more muscle, training and carbohydrate). Only for the muscle’s own work: muscle lacks the enzyme glucose-6-phosphatase, so it can’t release glucose into the blood.
      • Brain: only about 1 g, mostly in support cells (astrocytes). Heart, kidney and fat tissue also hold small amounts.

      Making it and breaking it

      • Glycogen synthase adds glucose to the free ends (α-1,4). Switched on after a meal, mainly by insulin.
      • Branching enzyme moves a short piece of chain to make a new branch (α-1,6).
      • Glycogen phosphorylase takes glucose off the free ends, one at a time. Switched on by glucagon (liver only) and adrenaline (liver and muscle), and by muscle work.
      • Debranching enzyme removes the branches so the breakdown can go on.

      The core protein glycogenin starts every granule: it attaches the first few glucose units to itself, and glycogen synthase builds on them.

      When the body uses it

      • After a meal: insulin tells liver and muscle to store glucose as glycogen.
      • Between meals and overnight: as blood sugar falls, glucagon tells the liver to break glycogen down and release glucose. Early in a fast, glycogen gives roughly a third to a half of the glucose the liver sends out; the rest is newly made.
      • Longer fasting: liver glycogen runs low after roughly a day or two without food; then the body makes new glucose, mostly in the liver (gluconeogenesis).
      • Exercise and stress: muscle burns its own glycogen; adrenaline also makes the liver release glucose.

        What sucrose is

        Sucrose is table sugar. Formula C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁ (molar mass 342.3 g/mol). It is a disaccharide: two simple sugars joined together, one glucose and one fructose.

        • Plants make it; the sugar we buy is purified from sugar cane or sugar beet.
        • Fruit, juice and honey also contain some sucrose, next to free glucose and fructose.

        The bond that holds it together

        • Glucose’s carbon 1 is joined through one oxygen to fructose’s carbon 2: an α-1→β-2 glycosidic bond (glucose in its α form, fructose in its β form).
        • These two carbons are the “anomeric” carbons, the ones that let a sugar ring open. Both are used up in the bond, so sucrose has no free end: it cannot open up, and it is a non-reducing sugar (it does not react in the classic Benedict or Fehling sugar test).
        • In sucrose the glucose is a six-membered ring (chair shape) and the fructose a five-membered ring.

        Splitting it: hydrolysis

        The body cannot absorb sucrose whole. The enzyme sucrase-isomaltase, on the surface of the cells lining the small intestine, splits it by adding one water molecule:

        C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁ + H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ (glucose) + C₆H₁₂O₆ (fructose)

        • The enzyme cuts the bond between glucose carbon 1 and the bridge oxygen. Experiments with heavy-oxygen water showed the water’s oxygen ends up on glucose carbon 1, and the glucose comes off as α-glucose.
        • The bridge oxygen stays with fructose and gets the water’s other hydrogen.
        • In the enzyme this happens in two quick steps (it holds the glucose for a moment); the animation shows it as one.

        The enzyme: sucrase-isomaltase

        • It sits on the brush border: the tiny finger-like microvilli on the gut lining cells.
        • It has two working halves: sucrase splits sucrose (and maltose); isomaltase splits maltose and the α-1,6 branch links left over from digested starch.
        • It splits sucrose right next to the doors (transporters) that take up the glucose and fructose; see the strip below.

        Absorption: from table sugar to the liver

        Hover over each part. The gut is on the left, the blood on the right. Drawn as a simple picture, not to scale.
        1. Sucrase splits sucrose into glucose and fructose on the brush border.
        2. SGLT1 takes glucose in together with two sodium ions (Na⁺); GLUT5 lets fructose in, no sodium needed.
        3. GLUT2 on the far side of the cell lets both sugars out into the blood.
        4. Blood from the gut goes first to the liver through the portal vein.

        When the enzyme is missing: in congenital sucrase-isomaltase deficiency, a rare inherited condition, sucrose (and maltose) cannot be split, so it travels on to the large intestine, pulls in water and is fermented by bacteria, causing stomach cramps, bloating, gas and diarrhea.

          What glucose is

          Formula C₆H₁₂O₆ (molar mass 180.2 g/mol). A simple sugar (monosaccharide) with six carbons.

          • In water almost all glucose is a ring: 5 carbons and 1 oxygen (a six-membered “pyranose” ring). Carbon 6 sticks out as a CH₂OH group.
          • Less than 1% (about 0.02%) is the open chain, where carbon 1 is an aldehyde group (–CHO). That is why glucose is called an aldose.
          • The ring is not flat: it has a chair shape. Flat hexagon drawings are a simplification.

          α and β glucose

          • When the ring closes, the new OH on carbon 1 can stick out at right angles to the ring (“axial”, drawn down in the usual flat drawing): α-glucose; or point out to the side (“equatorial”, drawn up): β-glucose.
          • In water the two keep turning into each other through the open chain. At balance there is about 36% α and 64% β.
          • β is a little more stable: in its chair, every OH group points out to the side.
          • In sucrose, starch and glycogen the glucose is joined in the α form. (Cellulose, which we cannot digest, is made of β-glucose.)

          In the body

          • Glucose is the body’s main blood sugar and fuel. A normal fasting level is below 100 mg/dL (5.6 mmol/L).
          • The brain uses about 120 g a day.
          • It comes from starch (long chains of glucose) and half of every sucrose. The liver can also make it (gluconeogenesis).
          • The gut takes it up through SGLT1 (with sodium) and passes it to the blood through GLUT2.
          • Extra glucose is stored as glycogen in liver and muscle.

            What fructose is

            Formula C₆H₁₂O₆, exactly the same atoms as glucose, but arranged differently (an isomer).

            • In the open chain, glucose has its C=O group at carbon 1 (an aldose); fructose has it at carbon 2 (a ketose).
            • So its ring closes between carbon 2 and carbon 5: a five-membered ring (4 carbons + 1 oxygen, a “furanose”), with CH₂OH groups on carbons 1 and 6 outside the ring.
            • In sucrose fructose is always this five-membered β ring. Free fructose in water is mostly a six-membered ring (about 68% β-pyranose, 22% β-furanose, smaller amounts of other forms).

            Where it comes from

            • Fruit and honey contain free fructose (honey is on average about 38% fructose and 30% glucose).
            • Table sugar is half fructose: every sucrose splits into one glucose and one fructose.
            • The gut takes it up through GLUT5, a door for fructose that needs no sodium, and passes it to the blood through GLUT2.

            How the body handles it

            • Fructose is processed mainly in the liver, and also in the small intestine and kidneys. Studies in mice show the gut wall itself converts much of a small dose; larger doses spill over to the liver.
            • The liver enzymes fructokinase and aldolase B handle it. Unlike glucose, this path is not slowed down when the cell already has enough energy.
            • The liver turns it into glucose, glycogen, lactate and, with large amounts, fat (which leaves the liver in VLDL).

              What insulin is

              A small protein hormone: 51 amino acids in two chains.

              • A chain: 21 amino acids. B chain: 30 amino acids.
              • Three disulfide bridges (two sulfur atoms bonded together) hold it in shape: two join the chains (A7–B7 and A20–B19), one is inside the A chain (A6–A11).
              • Mass about 5.8 kDa (5,808 daltons), about 30 times the mass of a glucose molecule.

              Made in the pancreas

              • Made by the beta cells in the islets (small cell clusters) of the pancreas.
              • It starts as one long chain, proinsulin: B chain – C-peptide (31 amino acids) – A chain. It folds and the bridges form.
              • Inside storage granules, enzymes (PC1/3, PC2 and carboxypeptidase E) cut out the C-peptide. What is left is insulin: the A and B chains, still joined by the bridges.
              • So insulin and C-peptide are released in equal amounts.

              Stored, then released

              • In the granules, six insulins pack together around two zinc ions (a hexamer). Each zinc is held by three histidines (B10). Press Storage form above to see it.
              • When blood glucose rises after a meal, the beta cells empty granules into the blood.
              • Once released, the hexamers fall apart. Insulin works as a single molecule (a monomer).
              • Blood from the pancreas goes to the liver first (portal vein).

              A short life in the blood

              • Its half-life in the blood is only about 4–6 minutes, so the level follows blood sugar closely.
              • The liver removes most of it, much of it on the very first pass. The kidneys clear a large part of the rest.
              • Cells take insulin in together with its receptor and break it down.

              What insulin does: opening the GLUT4 doors

              A muscle or fat cell, drawn as a simple picture (not to scale). Press Play, hover each part.
              1. Insulin binds the insulin receptor on the cell surface.
              2. The receptor is a tyrosine kinase: its two inside halves tag each other with phosphate (P).
              3. The signal passes on inside the cell: IRS → PI3K → Akt → AS160.
              4. GLUT4 doors, kept in small bubbles (vesicles) inside the cell, move to the surface and join it.
              5. Glucose flows in through GLUT4, from high (blood) to low (cell). No energy needed.

              Muscle and fat

              • Muscle burns the glucose or stores it as glycogen; fat cells use it to make and store fat.
              • Insulin also tells fat cells to keep their fat (it slows fat release).

              Liver

              • Glucose enters liver cells through GLUT2 doors that are always there; this does not need insulin.
              • Insulin tells the liver to store glucose as glycogen and to stop making and releasing glucose.

              Brain

              • The brain does not need insulin to take in glucose.
              • Glucose crosses the blood-brain barrier through GLUT1 and enters nerve cells through GLUT3, doors that are always open.

              Between meals

              • Insulin falls to a low background level. GLUT4 doors are pulled back inside, so muscle and fat take up less glucose.
              • The liver, helped by glucagon, releases glucose from glycogen and makes new glucose. Fat cells release fatty acids as fuel.

              When it goes wrong. Type 1 diabetes: the immune system destroys the beta cells, so the body makes almost no insulin; people need insulin every day. Type 2 diabetes (about 90–95% of cases): muscle, fat and liver respond poorly to insulin (insulin resistance), and over time the pancreas cannot make enough extra insulin.

                What glucagon is

                A small peptide hormone: 29 amino acids in one chain.

                • Its sequence: HSQGTFTSDYSKYLDSRRAQDFVQWLMNT (His1 … Thr29).
                • It has no cysteine, so no sulfur bridges, unlike insulin. It is held in shape only loosely.
                • Mass about 3.5 kDa (3,483 daltons), about 60% of an insulin.
                • Free in the blood it is a floppy chain. In the crystal, and when it sits on its receptor, it coils into a helix (spiral). Switch the shape above to compare.

                Made in the pancreas

                • Made by the alpha cells in the islets of the pancreas (the beta cells next to them make insulin).
                • It starts as a longer protein, proglucagon (160 amino acids, after a 20 amino acid start signal is cut off). In alpha cells the enzyme PC2 cuts glucagon out of it.
                • The gut’s L cells make the same proglucagon, but their enzyme PC1/3 cuts it differently, into GLP-1 and GLP-2. GLP-1 is the hormone copied by drugs like semaglutide.
                • Glucagon is stored in granules and released when the alpha cell gets the signal.

                When it is released

                • Low blood glucose: the strongest signal. It rises between meals and during fasting.
                • Exercise: it helps the liver keep up with the glucose working muscles take.
                • A protein-rich meal: amino acids raise glucagon (and insulin). Glucagon keeps blood sugar from dropping while insulin acts.
                • Nerves of the autonomic nervous system (as in stress) also act on the alpha cells.
                • Held back by high glucose, by insulin and somatostatin from neighbouring islet cells, and by the gut hormone GLP-1.

                A short life in the blood

                • Its half-life in the blood is only about 4–7 minutes (about 6 minutes measured in healthy adults), so the level changes fast.
                • Blood from the pancreas goes to the liver first (portal vein), so the liver gets the highest dose.
                • The kidneys remove most of it in people; the liver and enzymes in the blood (such as DPP-4) help break it down.
                • It is the opposite of insulin: insulin stores sugar after a meal, glucagon releases it between meals.

                What glucagon does: the liver releases glucose

                A liver cell, drawn as a simple picture (not to scale). Press Play, hover each part.
                1. Glucagon binds the glucagon receptor on a liver cell. It is a G protein coupled receptor: one chain that crosses the membrane seven times.
                2. The receptor switches on a G protein (Gs), which switches on adenylyl cyclase. That enzyme turns ATP into the signal cAMP.
                3. cAMP switches on protein kinase A (PKA).
                4. Glycogen breakdown (glycogenolysis): through phosphorylase kinase, PKA turns glycogen phosphorylase ON; it also turns glycogen synthase OFF. Glucose comes off the glycogen within minutes.
                5. New glucose (gluconeogenesis): PKA also turns up the genes and enzymes that build glucose from lactate, amino acids and glycerol. This takes over as glycogen runs low.
                6. The liver’s glucose-6-phosphatase frees the glucose, and it leaves through GLUT2 doors into the blood. Blood sugar goes up.

                Liver: the main target

                • The glucagon receptor sits mainly on liver cells (also in the kidneys and a few other places).
                • It breaks glycogen down, makes new glucose and stops the liver from storing glucose.
                • It also makes the liver burn more fatty acids, make ketones and make less new fat.

                Muscle: not affected

                • Muscle has no glucagon receptors, so glucagon does not touch muscle glycogen.
                • Muscle glycogen is broken down by adrenaline and by the muscle’s own work, and it is used only by that muscle.

                Fat tissue: little or none in people

                • In rats glucagon makes fat cells release fat. In people, normal glucagon levels have no clear effect on fat release, and glucagon receptors have not been shown on human fat cells.
                • Between meals fat cells release fatty acids mainly because insulin is low (and adrenaline helps).

                Brain

                • Glucagon does not feed the brain directly. The glucose the liver releases does.
                • That is why glucagon is the body’s first defence against low blood sugar.

                  What a fatty acid is

                  • A chain of carbon atoms with hydrogens on them: the tail. It does not mix with water.
                  • At one end, a carboxylic acid group (–COOH): the head. Its carbon is carbon 1.
                  • Short names count both: C16:0 means 16 carbons and 0 double bonds; C18:1 means 18 carbons and 1 double bond.
                  • In human fat, oleic, palmitic and linoleic acid make up about 85% of the fatty acids, oleic the most.

                  Saturated and unsaturated

                  • Palmitic acid (C₁₆H₃₂O₂): only single bonds between the carbons, so every carbon holds as many hydrogens as it can: saturated. The tail is a straight zig-zag; straight tails pack tightly. It melts at about 63 °C.
                  • Oleic acid (C₁₈H₃₄O₂): one cis double bond between carbons 9 and 10: unsaturated (mono-unsaturated). Both parts of the chain sit on the same side of the double bond, so the chain bends. Bent tails pack loosely: it melts at about 13–16 °C.
                  • Palmitic acid is the most common saturated fatty acid in the body (20–30% of all fatty acids). Oleic acid is the main fatty acid of olive oil (55–83%).

                  In the body

                  • A fuel: muscle and heart burn fatty acids for energy.
                  • When the body makes fat from extra sugar, fatty acid synthase builds palmitic acid (16 carbons).
                  • In the blood, free fatty acids ride on albumin, the main blood protein. Most fat in the blood, though, is in triglycerides inside lipoproteins (VLDL, chylomicrons).
                  • Cell membranes are made of phospholipids: glycerol with two fatty acids and a phosphate head. Cholesterol is a different kind of fat (a sterol, built of four rings), not made of fatty acids.

                    What glycerol is

                    Formula C₃H₈O₃ (92.09 g/mol), chemical name propane-1,2,3-triol: three carbons in a row, each with one OH group.

                    • The OH groups make it mix with water. On its own it is a clear, colourless, syrupy liquid (it turns solid below about 18 °C).
                    • Each OH can join one fatty acid through an ester bond. With all three joined, it becomes a triglyceride.

                    In the body

                    • It is the backbone of triglycerides (stored fat) and of the phospholipids in cell membranes.
                    • Cells build fat on glycerol 3-phosphate (glycerol carrying a phosphate group).
                    • When fat tissue breaks fat down between meals, the glycerol goes into the blood. The liver turns it into new glucose (gluconeogenesis).

                      What a triglyceride is

                      • One glycerol + three fatty acids. Each fatty acid is joined to one of glycerol’s carbons through an ester bond (–O–C(=O)–).
                      • Making one ester bond releases one water (a condensation): the fatty acid gives its OH, glycerol gives the H. Three bonds, three waters.
                      • The three fatty acids can be the same or different. POP, shown here, is one of the main fats in palm oil (about 30% of crude palm oil).
                      • Fats with mostly straight, saturated tails are solid at room temperature (butter, lard); fats with many bent tails are liquid oils (olive oil).

                      The body’s energy store

                      • Fat gives about 9 kcal per gram, sugar and starch about 4 kcal per gram.
                      • A white fat cell is mostly one big droplet of triglyceride (more than 90% of its content).
                      • After a meal, insulin makes fat tissue store fat. Between meals, with insulin low, fat cells cut it into fatty acids and glycerol and send them into the blood.

                      Building and breaking it in the body

                      • Building (liver, fat tissue, gut): the overall chemistry is the one shown. Cells do it in steps that use energy: each fatty acid is first activated (as acyl-CoA), then the enzymes GPAT, AGPAT and DGAT join them to glycerol 3-phosphate.
                      • Breaking in fat cells: three lipases, one fatty acid each: ATGL (prefers the middle one), then HSL, then MGL. Low insulin and adrenaline/noradrenaline switch this on; insulin blocks it. Glucagon has little effect on human fat cells at normal levels.
                      • In the gut, pancreatic lipase cuts only the two outer fatty acids: 2-monoglyceride + 2 fatty acids.
                      • In blood vessels, lipoprotein lipase unloads VLDL and chylomicrons. Freed fatty acids enter fat and muscle cells; fatty acids that fat tissue releases ride on albumin.

                        What cholesterol is

                        • A lipid: a waxy, fat-like substance that does not dissolve in water. But it is not built from fatty acids like the fat in fat cells: it is a sterol, four carbon rings joined together.
                        • C₂₇H₄₆O (386.7 g/mol): 27 carbons, 46 hydrogens and one oxygen.
                        • Pure cholesterol is a white, pearly solid that melts at about 148 °C and almost does not dissolve in water.
                        • Only one end is polar: the OH on carbon 3. All the rest is oily.

                        Its shape

                        • Rings A, B and C have six carbons, ring D has five. Joined like this they make a flat, stiff plate.
                        • One double bond, between carbons 5 and 6 in ring B.
                        • Two methyl groups (CH₃) stick up from the plate: C19 on carbon 10 and C18 on carbon 13. The OH points to the same side; that is what “3β” means.
                        • A branched 8-carbon tail (carbons 20 to 27) hangs off carbon 17 of ring D.
                        • 8 of its carbons are stereocentres, so 2⁸ = 256 arrangements are possible on paper; the body makes just this one.

                        How much, and where it comes from

                        • An adult of about 70 kg holds roughly 140–150 g of cholesterol. About a quarter of it is in the brain and spinal cord.
                        • The body makes about 1 g a day. Food adds less: adults in the US eat about 300 mg a day, and only about half of it is absorbed (from about 20% to 80%, depending on the person).
                        • Bile also pours about 1 g of cholesterol a day into the gut; part of it is taken back up.
                        • Almost every cell can make cholesterol. The liver makes part of it and is the hub: it takes cholesterol in from the blood, sends it out in VLDL, and gets rid of the extra into bile, as cholesterol and as bile acids.

                        What the body uses it for

                        • Cell membranes: most of the body’s cholesterol sits there, between the phospholipids. Its OH faces the water and its stiff rings make the membrane firmer and less leaky. In the brain most of it is in myelin, the wrap around nerve fibres.
                        • Bile acids: the liver turns about 0.5 g a day into bile acids, which help digest fat.
                        • Steroid hormones: cortisol, aldosterone and the sex hormones (estrogens, progesterone, testosterone), made in the adrenal glands, ovaries, testes and placenta.
                        • Vitamin D: the skin makes vitamin D₃ when sunlight (UVB) hits 7-dehydrocholesterol, the molecule one step before cholesterol on the same assembly line.

                        Free cholesterol and cholesteryl ester

                        • A cholesteryl ester is cholesterol with a fatty acid joined to its oxygen. The OH is gone, so it is oily all over: it cannot sit in a membrane surface and gathers in oily cores and droplets.
                        • In the blood, the enzyme LCAT makes it on HDL: it moves a fatty acid from lecithin (a phospholipid) onto cholesterol. Most ester in blood is made this way; the most common one is cholesteryl linoleate (about half).
                        • Inside cells, SOAT (also called ACAT) makes it from a fatty acid carried by coenzyme A, most often cholesteryl oleate (the example shown), and stores it in fat droplets.
                        • CETP passes cholesteryl esters from HDL to VLDL and LDL, and triglycerides the other way.

                        “Good” and “bad” cholesterol

                        • They are the same molecule. The nicknames describe the carrier, not the cholesterol.
                        • LDL (“bad”) carries cholesterol out to the tissues. When there are too many LDL particles, they get stuck in artery walls and build plaque.
                        • HDL (“good”) picks up extra cholesterol and brings it back to the liver.

                          Good to know
                          • Sugar travels alone. Glucose is dissolved in the blood plasma. HDL, LDL, VLDL and chylomicrons don’t carry sugar: they carry fats (triglycerides) and cholesterol, because fat doesn’t mix with water.
                          • “Good” and “bad” cholesterol are nicknames for the carriers, not two kinds of cholesterol. Too many LDL (ApoB) particles cause plaque. Low HDL often goes with insulin resistance, but raising HDL with drugs has not prevented heart attacks.
                          • Sugar into fat happens only a little on a normal diet; it rises sharply when you eat more carbohydrate (especially fructose) than you burn, and with insulin resistance.
                          • Body view is a simplified schematic: organ places and the main vessels are drawn to look believable, not to scale. Blood leaves the heart in arteries, passes the tissues and comes back in veins, through the lungs between the right and left heart; blood from the gut and pancreas goes to the liver first, through the portal vein. Many vessels (and the lymph) are left out.
